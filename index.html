Raffaele Errichiello, architect, researcher and designer

Architect, researcher and designer between the workshop and the lab: digital fabrication and engineered living material. A doctoral researcher in Umeå, Sweden, I design building parts in an engineered living material: a paste of biochar and wood waste, 3D printed, then grown through and bound by mycelium that stays alive inside it.

Biology as a design variable.

A part gets its qualities from three things I can design: the recipe, the printed geometry and the fungus itself. The toolpath decides where air gets in and where water stays, and the mycelium grows to match. Printing makes this precise and repeatable in a way moulds and hand packing are not: the same file gives the same part, layer for layer.

Printed. Grown. Welded.

“What if building parts could be printed, grown and welded together by a living organism?”

The first line of my 2026 paper in Architectural Intelligence, written with Julio C. Diarte Almada, and the question behind my doctorate.

Under the glass: demonstrator D2 being printed at the Material Practices Lab, Umeå, March 2026.

I trained as an architect in Naples and found my material in the north of Sweden.

My first structure was a frame of chestnut poles carrying three hammocks, built with fellow students in a field near Lecce in 2016. In 2019 I came to Umeå School of Architecture as an Erasmus student to research my master's thesis, and kept coming back. In the school workshop I started growing objects from residual materials from students' projects and the workshop's by-products, like timber, acrylic sheets, PVC and metal pipes, cardboard, sawdust and wood chips. That became a chair, the trophies for Sweden's national design awards (grown with Ellinor Werner), a letter N for Netflix, and then a doctorate.

Since 2024 I have been a doctoral candidate at the Material Practices Lab, supervised by Julio C. Diarte Almada and Gireesh Nair. I study how the printed geometry of a living material changes how it grows, how strong it becomes and how much it shrinks, so that AURORA, the grown structure I have been designing since 2019, can one day be built.

Coltivare strutture.Growing structures.

That was the title of my master's thesis, and it still describes the work. One line searches for form: structures that find their own shape, from three hammocks in Lecce to the shells of AURORA. A second grows matter, objects bound together by fungi. A third works with people: building, teaching and mapping with others. Since 2024 my doctorate has carried the matter line to the scale of building parts, while AURORA waits. When the doctorate ends, the three lines meet again in AURORA, printed and grown at full size.

“…a mycelium-based fiber-reinforced polymer suitable for 3D printing architectural structures.”

That was the material I said I wanted to develop next, made from the surplus of local manufacturers. A year later it became my PhD.

Doctoral research, 2024 to 2028

Geometry as a design parameterfor living materials

Why

Building parts that keep carbon,grown from what is left over

Construction needs materials that are low in carbon, can go back into a cycle, and can be shaped without much waste. Mycelium, the root network of a fungus, can bind wood residues into parts that are light and insulating, with no glue and no firing. Biochar, the charcoal left when wood waste is heated without air, holds its carbon in a stable form. In Umeå both come from what the forest and the workshops leave behind.

Printing these pastes needs no mould and allows shapes a mould cannot make. The difficulty is that the material is alive: wet paste slumps, growth follows air and water, and parts shrink as they dry. My doctorate asks whether the printed geometry itself can steer all three, so that a grown part keeps its shape and several parts can be joined into a larger element.

A printed toolpath does more than draw an outline. Its gaps let air in, its dense knots hold water, and the fungus grows accordingly. I design the pattern as a small climate for the fungus, then measure what it does to growth, strength and shrinkage, so that a printed part keeps its size and shape while it dries.

The Material Practices Lab, Umeå School of Architecture, with the grow tents on the right.

The material is a paste of biochar, sawdust from the school's wood workshop, cardboard pulp and spawn of Ganoderma lucidum, the reishi mushroom. A clay printer shapes it without a mould, the fungus binds it in about two weeks, and printed parts can be joined by growing them together instead of gluing them.

  1. Mix

    Biochar and wood fibres are sterilised, then kneaded with flour, binders and the reishi spawn.

  2. Print

    A clay printer lays the paste through an 8 mm nozzle, in one line that never stops.

  3. Grow

    Two weeks in a humid chamber at 24 °C, filmed by the night camera of the fermentation room, date stamp and all.

And a robot arm

Next to the clay printers, the lab has a UR15 robot arm fitted with a WASP extruder for liquid deposition printing. It reaches further than a printer bed, which matters once the parts grow towards the size of a building element.

A pattern borrowed from brain coral

The toolpath is a reaction-diffusion pattern, the chemistry that paints stripes on fish and folds the surface of brain corals, where the folds help water circulate. Printed, the folds become corridors for air between walls that carry the load.

Two numbers carry the design

Merging points, where the line touches itself and fuses, and porosity, the share of open space. Holding both steady from a 6 cm test cylinder to a 40 cm demonstrator lets small tests speak for large parts.

This is the real file

The drawing is the demonstrator's G-code, played back as the printer moves: 96 layers, one line that never stops, a little over five hours of printing at 1,200 mm per minute. The loupe rides on the nozzle.

Wide end up

The drawing shows the piece the way it stands. On the printer bed it is the other way round: the first layers are the widest, so the paste prints standing on them, and the grown piece is turned over.

Welded by the fungus

        

          
Fresh paste can only hold so much height and overhang, so demonstrator D1, 40 cm tall, was printed in three segments. After two weeks of growth, a thin layer of fresh paste went between them, and within seven days the mycelium had grown across the joints. The part was then left to dry in air for at least four weeks.

          
No glue, no screws. The joint became an essay, below.

Anatomy of a Living Joint

          
UOU Scientific Journal 10, In Detail, December 2025

        

        

          
The joint between the printed segments became an essay in the UOU Scientific Journal of the University of Alicante. Its pattern comes from a brain coral: the ridges leave open channels where air reaches the fungus and closed wells where moisture stays, so hyphae from both segments grow across the seam and weld them without glue.

          
The essay follows the joint through the scales, the way Charles and Ray Eames did in Powers of Ten: from the joint in centimetres down to a 30 mm sample and to hyphae a few micrometres wide, then back out along a column, to a 9 metre pavilion and a city block whose buildings share a fungal network underground. Read the essay, open access.

Does it hold?

        

          
Demonstrator D2 holds me sitting down and standing on one leg. The compression tests are more careful: on one day in April 2025 we crushed samples of different recipes, shapes and sizes, one after another.

          
The first results suggest the brain-coral toolpath can make parts stronger, but there are still too few samples to be sure. The next round of tests is designed to settle that.

        

      

    

  










  

    
Now

    

      
Early findings,shared with colleagues

      
The results so far are early ones: printed parts that keep their size within a few percent as they dry, segments that grow into one piece in a week, and a toolpath that may make parts stronger. I put them in front of other researchers while they are still early, on a table and in talks, and their questions go back into the work.

    

    
    
Every printed piece so far on one table at EURAU26 in Umeå, June 2026, where I presented the paper on biochar-mycelium composites for circular construction in northern Sweden and chaired a session.

Rest areas designed and built with fellow students in Sergio Pone's Architecture Technology course, University of Naples Federico II, for Masseria Ospitale, Lecce, summer 2016.

      

        
        
      

      
The frame in two versions, June 2016.

      

        

          
The name has three capital Ms for its three poles, the three mazze, as we call them in Neapolitan. The Masseria had asked the university for places to rest, and we designed them from what was already in its store, so each frame cost nothing. Three chestnut poles cross at one joint, bound with rope, and carry three hammocks between them, with cables tying their tips together. Geometry gives the frame both its strength and its look.

          
The poles hold each other up through reciprocal forces, and the frame needs weight to stay balanced. With one person in it, it stands only because of the anchors at its feet; the more people sit in it, the steadier it gets. It began with a few printed pages on tensegrity and a sketch on squared paper. We drew the parts in Rhino, tested a table-top model and raised the frames in a field among olive trees. It was the first structure I watched find its shape under load, and the first one I built together with others.

First experiments with mycelium

    

  

  

    

      
2021to 2022
Naples, Umeå

Alongside the thesis, November 2021 to May 2022: growing Pleurotus ostreatus, the oyster mushroom, at home near Naples, a visit to the farm Il Rivo above Sorrento, then the first sterile cultures in Umeå.

      
      
Mixing day, 19 January 2022, filmed from above: the substrates weighed and mixed, the bags filled and taped, sterilised in a pressure cooker, then inoculated with spawn.

      

        

          
          
        

        

          
Before designing with a living material I had to learn to grow it. I mixed substrates in different proportions, filled bags and jars, sterilised them and inoculated them with spawn, then watched which mixes the mycelium took over, which stayed bare and which grew mould.

          
In January 2022 we saw the first fruit at Il Rivo, a permaculture farm in Sant'Agata sui Due Golfi above Sorrento, where a jar of Pleurotus ostreatus and a block of lion's mane, Hericium erinaceus, were prepared with the help of Chiara Sente. Until the REishi chair, everything I grew myself was Pleurotus ostreatus. From the chair on, it is Ganoderma.

2023 to 2024, Umeå and Stockholm

      
A letter N for Netflix

    

  

  

    

      
2023to 2024
Umeå and Stockholm
Matter

      


      
Commission from Open Studio Stockholm for Netflix's Stockholm office, December 2023 to March 2024. Shelving by Noas Sweden. The plywood panel was made with experts from the workshops of Umeå School of Architecture and Umeå Institute of Design.

      

        
        

          

            
The material was the workshop's leftovers: coconut fibre, wood pulp and cellulose from discarded cardboard, colonised by reishi spawn into a light, solid letter. It grew in moulds of perforated acrylic, so the fungus could breathe, and was sprayed red at the end. I also designed the 3D printed brackets that fix it to the wave-shaped shelving made by Noas Sweden.

            
It was the first time a client asked for the material rather than a drawing.

          

          

            
            
          

        

      

      

        
        
        
      

      
The grown material close up and the letters on black in the studio, March 2024; then the making of the letters, December 2023 to March 2024: the design, the moulds, the reishi growing through the holes, the stands in the spray booth and the drying oven.

Tutor at ProtoLAB, the design and build summer workshops of the Faculty of Architecture, Wrocław University of Science and Technology, July 2024 and July 2025. ProtoLAB 2025 at Wrocław Tech.

      
      

        

          
In 2024, with the students of my group and the other tutors, I built an armadillo: a vault of discrete timber arches carrying square boxes filled with straw, bamboo and pine cones, places for bees and other insects to nest.

          
In 2025 I tutored, with Javier Sánchez Merina of the Universidad de Alicante, a group of students who wanted to build a telescopic canoe. Its strength comes from double curvature and a thin skin of laminated wood.

2024, Barcelona, and in the future

      
AURORA

    

  

  

    

      
2024
Barcelona, and in the future
FormDoctoral research

      


      
Adaptive Units for Rational, Organic and Resilient Architecture. Paper at SIGraDi 2024, Barcelona. Not yet built.

      
      

        
From the SIGraDi 2024 paper.

        

          
AURORA is where the form line has been heading since the snow shells of 2020. It took shape in the thesis, was rebuilt in steel and rope as MK3 in 2022, and was published at SIGraDi 2024: shells shaped like soap films, found with a physics solver and checked with structural analysis, with a reaction-diffusion pattern moving material to where the loads are. Hexagonal units combine to fit a site and the people who use it.

          
It has not been built. My doctorate is the groundwork: before AURORA can be printed in a living material, I need to know how its geometry changes growth, strength and dimensional stability. Building it is the step after the defence.

Reaction‑diffusion pattern

        

          
Two substances react and spread at different speeds, and from an almost even start, stripes, spots and folds settle out on their own. Alan Turing described the mechanism in 1952, and these are often called Turing patterns: defined by mathematics, and found again in nature, from the stripes on a zebrafish to the ridges of brain coral.

          
AURORA uses this pattern to lay out its material, and the demonstrators use it in their toolpaths. The one above grows live in your browser from a few random seeds, so you will never see the same pattern twice.

Work with me

    

      
What we couldgrow together

      
I am looking for partners, sites and good questions. If one of these sounds like you, write to me.

    

    
    
      
  • A pavilion that grows in place
    AURORA at full size, printed in the living composite in sections, grown, and welded on site. I am looking for a festival, a school or a park that wants to host the first one.
    • 
      
  • Your residue as a material
    Sawdust, pulp, bark, fibre or biochar: if your production leaves something behind, I would like to test whether a fungus can make it printable.
    • 
      
  • Living material, measured properly
    Mycologists, soil ecologists and materials scientists: how should a living building part age, decay and be tested? I would like to answer that with you.
    • 
      
  • A studio or workshop week
    From petri dish to printed object in five days, for students of architecture, design or biology.
    • 
      
  • An exhibition that keeps growing
    Printed pieces that change during the show, with the fungus still at work, for galleries, design weeks and science festivals.
    • 
      
  • Research positions
    I am open to research collaborations now, and to postdoctoral and research positions after my defence in 2028.
